Learned parts propose and verifiable code decides what is allowed, like an interlock. A parsing error can still let through a timetable that breaks what the student meant (Section~\ref{sec:res-pipeline}), which is why the student confirms the conditions first, but no model error can produce a clash.

\subsection{Parsing the Sentence}\label{sec:parse}

Rules split the sentence, a classifier labels each clause, and rules read the values; in a four-sentence pilot, untrained Qwen models that had to return every condition at once dropped or invented some.

We compared keyword rules, TF-IDF (term-frequency, inverse-document-frequency) character $n$-grams with logistic regression, Qwen2.5-7B with no training and two versions of a detailed prompt (v2 a revision of v1), and Qwen2.5-7B fine-tuned with QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}. Training data came from a teacher-student loop: Qwen2.5-7B generated \sClauses{} labelled clauses in English, Chinese and mixed language, Claude graded \sGraded{} of them and wrote \sHardOne{} harder ones, and a second round added \sHardTwo{} Claude-written clauses aimed at round~1's two weakest labels, giving \sTrainTwo{} training clauses (\sClaudeTrainPct\% written by Claude) and \sValTwo{} validation clauses after rejected ones were removed. Fine-tuning took \loraMinutes{} minutes on one RTX~4090 Laptop GPU (Appendix~\ref{app:model}); the model was then quantised to 4-bit (Q4\_K\_M) and served through Ollama. Round~2 replaced round~1 under a rule written before it was scored.

When unsure, the parser asks instead of guessing: when a second, cheaper classifier disagrees, when a clause could be hard or soft, or when a busy time has no day or hour. The student's answer overrides the model. It may also offer up to two unmentioned limits, such as a lunch break.

The scheduler lists every valid timetable exactly: it searches the groups with the fewest options first, removes every class clashing with a chosen one, and backtracks as soon as a group has no class left; pairwise clashes are precomputed as bit sets, identical classes searched once. Hard limits (clashes, unavailable times, full classes, walking time when set, day and hour limits, lunch) are checked on real dates; $G$, $D$ and $O$ are updated incrementally and the non-dominated timetables kept (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}). Five units (\ttFive{} valid timetables) take \ttFiveSec{}~s on a Mac. If nothing is feasible, it names the clashing activities and offers the timetables that break the fewest limits, stating each; a clash is never allowed. The search order affects only speed; every valid timetable is found. On 65 random problems, 40 with day, hour and lunch limits, it matched a slower reference search in counts and measures.

\subsection{Ranking the Candidates}\label{sec:rank}

Claude wrote \sPersonas{} student personas across ten situations (commuters, workers, parents and others), each a sentence with its intended conditions. For each persona the real scheduler produced candidates, and Qwen2.5-14B, playing the persona, chose between pairs shown side by side. Each pair was asked in both orders and kept only if the answers agreed (\tKept{} of \tAsked{}, \tConsistent\%); when two timetables were almost equal on the persona's main concern, code decided by dominance or called a tie. Claude audited \tClaudeN{} of the teacher's labels blind, agreed with \tClaudeAgree\%, and replaced the \sReplaced{} it disagreed with.

The deployed model was fitted to the first \rDeployPersonas{} training personas (\rDeployTeacher{} teacher pairs plus \rDeployCode{} dominance pairs, where one timetable is no worse on any measure, code-labelled, Table~\ref{tab:data}), with regularisation chosen by persona-grouped 5-fold cross-validation. On the \sTestPersonas{} held-out personas the deployed model agreed with \rHeldOne\% of the labels (\rHeldTeacherLR\% of the pairs Qwen judged, against \rHeldTeacherEq\% for equal weights). A refit on all \sTrainPersonas{} training personas scored \rHeldTwo\%, no better, so by a rule set beforehand it was not shipped. A random forest and a small neural network scored within one point in cross-validation, so we kept logistic regression, a ``glass-box'' model in the Week~11 lecture's sense~\cite{engg2112_w11}: each score splits into per-feature contributions, in a 4~KB weights file.

\subsection{Reasons, Evaluation and Environment}\label{sec:explain}

Each pick's reason is built from facts about its dated meetings and from the ranker's contributions (Figure~\ref{fig:app}b). The validation split (mostly Qwen-generated) and two development test sets written in the coordinating Claude session guided every design choice. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals over sentences or personas, paired when comparing methods. After the freeze only the wording of the parser's notes and questions changed; the model weights, the ranker and the search logic did not, so the parsing and ranking scores are for the frozen models. The one addition, the closest-timetable fallback, is scored separately in Section~\ref{sec:res-pipeline}.
