\subsection{Parsing and Asking Back}\label{sec:res-parse}

Against the keyword-rule baseline of O1, the deployed model was \pDiffKeyC{} points better on clauses and \pDiffKeyS{} on whole sentences (paired 95\% CIs \pDiffKeyCLo{} to \pDiffKeyCHi{} and \pDiffKeySLo{} to \pDiffKeySHi; Figure~\ref{fig:results}). Fine-tuning made the difference: the same model untrained was no better than keyword rules, and training added \pDiffZeroC{} points on clauses (CI \pDiffZeroCLo{} to \pDiffZeroCHi) and \pDiffZeroS{} on whole sentences. Round~2 beat round~1 by \pDiffOneC{} points on clauses (CI \pDiffOneCLo{} to \pDiffOneCHi), but on whole sentences the difference is within noise. The offline fallback, TF-IDF with logistic regression, was chosen on validation data (\pLinVal\% of clauses against \pKeyVal\% for keywords, mostly Chinese or mixed clauses) but was no better than keyword rules on the all-English exam; we did not switch. Splitting was exactly right for only \pSplit\% of sentences, the same for every classifier (a different split often still gives the same conditions), and most remaining sentence errors come from the rule-based steps.

The parser questioned \cAsked\% of clauses (\cAskedN, of which \cCovered{} were wrong), and \cSentWithQ\% of sentences got at least one question or offer. The questions covered \cCovered{} of the \cWrong{} wrong clauses, and for \cOption{} of them the correct reading was an option, so if students picked it clause accuracy would reach about \cProjected\% (a projection; no student answered).

\subsection{Ranking}\label{sec:res-rank}

The trained logistic regression agreed with the independent answers on \rLR\% of pairs and recovered \rLRTop{} of their top three (Table~\ref{tab:rank}); equal weights reached \rEq\% and \rEqTop. The paired difference is \rDiffEq{} points (95\% CI \rDiffEqLo{} to \rDiffEqHi), so the trained model is level with equal weights: a rule that ignores the student's stated preferences, while still respecting their limits, does almost as well. Most of the deployed model's training pairs (\rDomShare\%) were dominance pairs, which likely push it towards the same choices as equal weights. The student terms helped against the teacher's labels (removing them cost \rAblInteraction{} points on 20 held-out personas) but not on the exam: on held-out teacher labels the cares-only rule beat equal weights (\rHeldTeacherCare\% against \rHeldTeacherEq\% of the pairs Qwen judged), on the exam the order reversed (\rCares\% against \rEq\%), so the ranker learned the teacher's weight on the stated concern, which our prompt, stating it first, may have strengthened. Qwen2.5-14B judging pairs directly did worse (\rQwen\%), partly because it saw each pair once and chose the first-shown timetable \rQwenFirst\% of the time; teacher labels used both orders.

On the \rAgreePersonas{} personas answered twice, the two answerers agreed on \rAgree\% of pairs where both chose a side, so the model's \rLR\% is near the ceiling this exam can measure, which also limits how clearly any method can beat equal weights. Against the teacher's labels, accuracy was flat beyond 60 training personas (Figure~\ref{fig:appendix}b), so more labels of the same kind are unlikely to help; whether better labels would is untested.

\subsection{Whole Pipeline and Reasons}\label{sec:res-pipeline}

On the parsing exam, \plExact\% of sentences (\plExactN{} of \sExamS) were parsed to exactly the intended conditions. Of the \plPlannable{} sentences whose parsed conditions allowed a timetable (\figStudents{} under the intended ones), \plViol{} (\plViolPct\%) received a recommendation that broke a stated condition. In \plViolFix{} of them a clarifying question had already offered the fix, mostly hard-or-soft misreadings such as ``no 9ams please'' (Figure~\ref{fig:app}), which the annotator treated as a limit and the parser as a preference; the rest came from splitting or number reading, such as ``no classes before 10 no classes after 4 no classes on friday'' written without commas. An independent re-check of the dated meetings found no clash in the \plPicksN{} recommended timetables or the \plNearestPicks{} closest ones offered when nothing fit. The first pick matched the intended conditions' pick in \plSameFirst\% of the \plSameFirstOf{} sentences with both. Under the intended conditions, \plNoFit{} sentences (\plNoFitPct\%) had no timetable meeting every limit; the first closest timetable offered broke \plNoFitBreaks{} limits on average (a post-freeze fallback).

All \exPicks{} template reasons for the picks under the intended conditions passed the automatic fact check, as designed, and \exMain\% named the ranker's largest contribution first. The check's real use was on Qwen2.5-7B rewrites: only \exRewrite\% passed, and \exManualWrong{} of \exManualOf{} passing ones Claude reviewed were still false, so the site shows templates only.

\subsection{Issues Faced}\label{sec:issue}

With the first ranking prompt, Qwen2.5-7B gave the same answer in both orders for only \tPilotFirst{} of \tPilotN{} pilot pairs. A side-by-side table, the main concern stated first and code-decided near-ties raised this to \tPilotSevenOK{} of the \tPilotAsked{} pairs put to the 7B model and \tPilotAskedOK{} of those put to Qwen2.5-14B, the teacher (\tPilotFinal{} of \tPilotN{} with the code-decided ones). A side effect remains: code-decided near-ties prefer fewer one-class days, so a student who cares most about waiting is sometimes offered slightly more waiting.
