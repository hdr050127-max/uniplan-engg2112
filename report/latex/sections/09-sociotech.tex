\section{Stakeholders and Socio-Technical Issues at Scale}\label{sec:socio}

\textbf{Stakeholders.} Students gain time and a clearer view of trade-offs; the timetabling office gains demand data but faces more concentrated demand; coordinators see class sizes shift; disability and carer services gain a channel for access needs in students' own words.

\textbf{Demand would concentrate.} Thousands of students with similar wishes, such as no Friday classes, would be steered to the same classes, which would fill sooner; students who enrol last may lose most. A deployed version should show seat numbers and spread recommendations across equivalent classes.

\textbf{Accuracy may differ between students.} We tested only English written by an AI. Chinese and mixed-language clauses are most of the training data but were never tested, and second-language writers may be misread more often, so accuracy should be measured per group before launch, following the Week~11 lecture's fairness principles~\cite{engg2112_w11}.

\textbf{The sentence is sensitive.} Students explain their week with a job, a disability or a family member who needs care. The Week~6 lecture warns about leakage and re-identification of personal data~\cite{engg2112_w06}. UniPlan runs locally and needs no name or ID; a service should discard sentences after use and not send them to third parties.

\textbf{People over-trust automated advice.} Users of decision aids tend to accept the first suggestion and miss its errors~\cite{parasuraman2010}; notes and questions reduce but do not remove it.

\textbf{The model learned an AI's preferences.} The ranker was distilled~\cite{hinton2015distill} from Qwen2.5-14B playing students, so any bias in how these models imagine students, such as everyone wanting fewer days, is now in the weights; only real students can reveal it.

\textbf{Accountability.} A student who misses a class or a shift because of a recommendation bears the cost, so a service needs a named owner and a way to report errors.
